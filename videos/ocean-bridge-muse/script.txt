How do you build a bridge this big in the ocean, when there is no solid ground in sight?

The answer starts below the water. Here is one way to build a coastal bridge.

First, survey vessels map the seabed, while ground investigations reveal what lies beneath it.

Engineers design deep foundations to transfer the bridge's weight into suitable ground.

For this example, a steel casing helps hold a drilled shaft open through water and unstable soil.

A drill removes material inside. Then the bottom is cleaned before the reinforcing cage goes in.

But how do you pour concrete into a hole full of water?

A pipe called a tremie delivers concrete near the bottom, instead of dropping it through the water.

Its outlet stays buried in fresh concrete as the concrete level rises, displacing the water above.

After the concrete gains strength and passes checks, the foundation can support the pier above it.

Meanwhile, large bridge components are made on land, where construction is easier to control.

Barges carry them out. Crews wait for suitable wind, waves, and currents.

A crane lifts each girder onto prepared supports. Connections and the deck follow.

The finished bridge still needs protection against corrosion and erosion around its foundations.

Not every ocean bridge uses this method. Water depth, ground conditions, and bridge design change the solution.

But the principle stays the same: carry the road's weight through the structure, into the ground below.
