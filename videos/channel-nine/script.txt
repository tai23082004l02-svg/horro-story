The first time I heard my own voice on channel nine, it was begging me not to open the door. Not a voice that sounded like mine. Mine. The same breathless catch I get when I try not to panic. Then somebody knocked. Three slow knocks. I was alone at Blackpine Relay, a little communications station above thirty miles of forest. My shift had started an hour earlier. I had already done the radio check. Blackpine Relay. Daniel Reed. Night shift. Now the same speaker was saying my name back to me. Outside, rain crawled down the window. Inside, the wall clock said twelve thirteen. The voice gave a time four minutes later. Twelve seventeen. And then it said something I could not understand until much later. Whatever happens, don't let her answer.

There was a laminated instruction card beside the radio. The official side explained how to log emergency traffic. On the back, somebody had written one sentence in black marker. If you hear yourself, do not answer. I thought it was a joke left for the new guy. I pressed the microphone anyway. Who is this? The red light stayed on after I released the button. From the speaker came a soft breath. Then my voice said, You just made it easier. I pulled the microphone plug. The voice continued. In four minutes you're going to break the white mug. You're going to find a red key behind the battery. And you're going to call your sister. I looked at the mug beside my hand. I had not told anyone at work about Lena. The telephone on the desk began to ring.

I let it ring until it stopped. The radio clicked. You should have picked up, my voice said. She thinks you're angry. I stood so quickly my chair caught the table. The mug fell. It struck the floor in three pieces. The clock said twelve seventeen. Four minutes. I told myself somebody had cameras in the room. That somebody was watching me and playing a recording. I searched above the shelves, under the desk, inside the dead security monitor. Nothing. Then I saw the battery at the back of the radio rack. Behind it was a red key. One tooth was broken off. A little paper tag had gone soft with age. On it was a drawing of the steel cabinet in the back hallway. From outside came another knock. This time it was at the window. The glass was ten feet above the ground.

I called Lena on the landline. My cell phone had no service up there. She answered on the second ring, half asleep. I told her I needed her to stay on the phone. Not to drive up. Just talk. While she asked what was wrong, the radio made a sound like someone clearing their throat. A woman's voice came through. Lena's voice. Half a second before she spoke into the telephone, the radio said the same words. Daniel, are you hurt? I hung up. The radio went silent. For the first time, the thing outside stopped knocking. I understood enough to be afraid of the wires. The antenna cable ran through the rear wall. The telephone cable disappeared into the same steel cabinet. I followed them with my flashlight. Something wet brushed the other side of the wall, keeping pace with me.

The red key fitted the cabinet. Inside were the antenna switch, the telephone junction, and a small backup power supply. Scratched into the metal was another warning. It follows the open line. My voice returned from the front room. Don't touch the antenna. I'm trying to help you. I turned the switch a quarter turn. The voice stretched, thin and distant. Outside, something dragged a fingernail down the window. I turned the switch back. The scratching stopped. Then my voice whispered, Look at me. I should not have looked. A man stood beyond the glass. My jacket. My haircut. My face. But he was smiling with his mouth closed, and the shape of the smile moved under his skin. His right hand was pressed against the glass. I have a scar across my left palm. His scar was on the wrong hand.

The station lights went out. The radio did not. Its green lamp burned on the backup battery. The man outside tilted his head. In the dark, I heard him speak from the radio, from the telephone, and from the other side of the window. All at once. Cut the power, he said. I opened the cabinet again. My flashlight found two thick black cables. One fed the antenna. One fed the telephone junction. The red key shook between my fingers. From the desk, Lena called my name. The receiver was still on its cradle. Please, Daniel. Pick up. I reached for the antenna connector. The handle of the front door began to turn. Slowly. I pulled the cable free. Every speaker in the station screamed with my voice. I dropped the flashlight. In the blackness, something heavy struck the front door. Then there was silence.

I waited until I could hear the rain again. The radio light was dead. The telephone was silent. Nothing stood at the window. I left through the back, climbed into my car, and kept the headlights off until I reached the road. When the dashboard radio lit up, I tore its fuse out. I drove down the mountain with both hands locked on the wheel. At the first place my phone found service, I called Lena. It rang once. Then I ended the call before she could answer. Whatever happens, don't let her answer. I finally understood that sentence. I had thought the voice was warning me about the future. Maybe it was only teaching me what to do. Maybe it had needed a voice, and a reason for me to open another line. I drove the rest of the way without touching the phone.

Lena's porch light was on when I arrived. Her front door stood open. I stopped at the bottom of the steps. She appeared in the hallway wearing her blue sleep shirt. She looked past me, toward the road. Then back at my face. How did you get outside? she asked. I told her not to say another word. From the upstairs bedroom, my voice called down. Lena? Who are you talking to? She began to turn. I caught her wrist and pulled her onto the porch. Behind her, the telephone started ringing. She looked at the phone in my hand. The screen was black. I wasn't calling. Upstairs, bare feet crossed the floor. Slow steps. Then a man laughed with my voice. I had disconnected the antenna. I had never touched the telephone line. At the top of the stairs, something knocked three times.
